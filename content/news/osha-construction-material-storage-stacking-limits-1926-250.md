Stacks of brick, block, bagged material and lumber are on almost every construction site. OSHA's construction rules
set limits on how that material is stored in 29 CFR 1926.250, "General requirements for storage," the first section of
Subpart H (Materials Handling, Storage, Use, and Disposal). This explainer reads the section as it stands in the eCFR
(text current to November 20, 2026). It is an explainer, not a rule change: the eCFR lists the section's most recent
amendment at 84 FR 21577 (May 14, 2019). It covers construction work under Part 1926, not general industry workplaces
under Part 1910.

## What is the basic rule for stacked material?

1926.250(a)(1) states: "All materials stored in tiers shall be stacked, racked, blocked, interlocked, or otherwise
secured to prevent sliding, falling or collapse."

The rule names several methods and ends with "or otherwise secured," so the test is the result: the stack must not
slide, fall or collapse.

## Do floor load limits have to be posted?

Usually, yes. 1926.250(a)(2)(i) states: "The weight of stored materials on floors within buildings and structures shall
not exceed maximum safe load limits." Paragraph (a)(2)(ii) then requires employers to "conspicuously post maximum safe
load limits of floors within buildings and structures, in pounds per square foot, in all storage areas."

There are stated exceptions. Posting is not required "when the storage area is on a floor or slab on grade," and it is
"not required for storage areas in all single-family residential structures and wood-framed multi-family residential
structures." The weight limit in (a)(2)(i) itself has no such exception.

## How far from openings and edges must material be kept?

1926.250(b)(1) applies to material stored inside buildings under construction. It "shall not be placed within 6 feet of
any hoistway or inside floor openings, nor within 10 feet of an exterior wall which does not extend above the top of the
material stored."

So the 10-foot distance applies only where the exterior wall does not extend above the top of the stored material.

## How high can brick and block be stacked?

1926.250(b)(6) and (b)(7) give specific numbers:

- **Brick:** "Brick stacks shall not be more than 7 feet in height. When a loose brick stack reaches a height of 4 feet,
  it shall be tapered back 2 inches in every foot of height above the 4-foot level."
- **Masonry block:** "When masonry blocks are stacked higher than 6 feet, the stack shall be tapered back one-half block
  per tier above the 6-foot level."

Note the difference: brick has a stated maximum height of 7 feet, and the taper rule applies to a "loose brick stack."
The block rule sets a taper above 6 feet but states no maximum height.

## How high can lumber be piled?

1926.250(b)(8) has four lumber rules:

- "Used lumber shall have all nails withdrawn before stacking."
- "Lumber shall be stacked on level and solidly supported sills."
- "Lumber shall be so stacked as to be stable and self-supporting."
- "Lumber piles shall not exceed 20 feet in height provided that lumber to be handled manually shall not be stacked more
  than 16 feet high."

## What about bags, pipe and steel?

- **Bagged material:** 1926.250(b)(4) states: "Bagged materials shall be stacked by stepping back the layers and
  cross-keying the bags at least every 10 bags high."
- **Cylindrical material:** 1926.250(b)(9) states: "Structural steel, poles, pipe, bar stock, and other cylindrical
  materials, unless racked, shall be stacked and blocked so as to prevent spreading or tilting."
- **Incompatible material:** 1926.250(b)(3) states: "Noncompatible materials shall be segregated in storage."

## Can material be stored on scaffolds?

Only what is needed right away. 1926.250(b)(5) states: "Materials shall not be stored on scaffolds or runways in excess
of supplies needed for immediate operations."

## What about silos, hoppers and tanks?

1926.250(b)(2) states: "Each employee required to work on stored material in silos, hoppers, tanks, and similar storage
areas shall be equipped with personal fall arrest equipment meeting the requirements of subpart M of this part."

## What else does 1926.250 cover?

- **Aisles:** 1926.250(a)(3) requires that "Aisles and passageways shall be kept clear to provide for the free and safe
  movement of material handling equipment or employees," and kept in good repair.
- **Different levels:** under (a)(4), where road or working levels differ, "means such as ramps, blocking, or grading
  shall be used to ensure the safe movement of vehicles between the two levels."
- **Housekeeping:** 1926.250(c) states: "Storage areas shall be kept free from accumulation of materials that constitute
  hazards from tripping, fire, explosion, or pest harborage."
- **Dockboards:** 1926.250(d) requires portable and powered dockboards to be "strong enough to carry the load imposed on
  them," portable dockboards to be secured against slipping and to have handholds or other effective means for safe
  handling, and "positive protection" to keep railroad cars from moving while dockboards or bridge plates are in
  position.

## What this means for OSHA study

The numbers to know from 1926.250 are: 6 feet from hoistways and inside floor openings; 10 feet from an exterior wall
that does not extend above the top of the stored material; brick stacks no more than 7 feet, with loose stacks tapered
back 2 inches per foot above 4 feet; block tapered back one-half block per tier above 6 feet; bags stepped back and
cross-keyed at least every 10 bags high; and lumber piles no higher than 20 feet, or 16 feet for lumber handled
manually. Floor load limits are posted in pounds per square foot, with the exceptions stated in (a)(2)(ii).
