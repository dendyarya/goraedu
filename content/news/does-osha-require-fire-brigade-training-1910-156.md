Some plants, refineries and large facilities have their own team of employees trained to fight fires. A common question
is whether OSHA requires one, and what the rules are once a workplace has one. The answer is in OSHA's general industry
rule 29 CFR 1910.156, "Fire brigades," in Subpart L (Fire Protection). This explainer reads the section as it stands in
the eCFR (text current to November 20, 2026); it does not describe a rule change. It covers general industry workplaces
under Part 1910.

## Does OSHA require an employer to have a fire brigade?

1910.156 does not say an employer must form one. Its scope, in 1910.156(a)(1), is the organization, training and
protective equipment of fire brigades "whenever they are established by an employer."

The definition is in 1910.155(c)(18): a fire brigade (also called a private or industrial fire department) "means an
organized group of employees who are knowledgeable, trained, and skilled in at least basic fire fighting operations."

Under 1910.156(a)(2), the section applies to "fire brigades, industrial fire departments and private or contractual type
fire departments." It does "not apply to airport crash rescue or forest fire fighting operations."

## What must be in writing?

1910.156(b)(1) requires the employer to "prepare and maintain a statement or written policy which establishes the
existence of a fire brigade." The statement covers the basic organizational structure; the type, amount and frequency of
training; the expected number of members; and the functions the brigade is to perform at the workplace. It must be
available for inspection by the Assistant Secretary and by employees or their designated representatives.

## How often must fire brigade members be trained?

1910.156(c)(1) requires training and education "commensurate with those duties and functions that fire brigade members
are expected to perform," given "before they perform fire brigade emergency activities." Leaders and training instructors
must receive training that is "more comprehensive" than that of the general membership.

The frequency is in 1910.156(c)(2): "All fire brigade members shall be provided with training at least annually. In
addition, fire brigade members who are expected to perform interior structural fire fighting shall be provided with an
education session or training at least quarterly."

Under 1910.156(c)(4), the employer must also tell members about special hazards, such as flammable liquids and gases,
toxic chemicals, radioactive sources and water reactive substances, and develop written procedures for those situations
that are included in the training.

## What counts as interior structural fire fighting?

The difference between members who fight small fires and those who go inside matters throughout the section. The two
key definitions in 1910.155(c) are:

- **Incipient stage fire** (c)(26): "a fire which is in the initial or beginning stage and which can be controlled or
  extinguished by portable fire extinguishers, Class II standpipe or small hose systems without the need for protective
  clothing or breathing apparatus."
- **Interior structural fire fighting** (c)(28): "the physical activity of fire suppression, rescue or both, inside of
  buildings or enclosed structures which are involved in a fire situation beyond the incipient stage."

## Who is physically allowed to serve?

Under 1910.156(b)(2), the employer must make sure employees expected to do interior structural fire fighting are
physically capable of the duties. It "shall not permit employees with known heart disease, epilepsy, or emphysema, to
participate in fire brigade emergency activities unless a physician's certificate of the employees' fitness to
participate in such activities is provided."

## How often must the equipment be inspected?

1910.156(d) states that the employer "shall maintain and inspect, at least annually, fire fighting equipment" and that
"Portable fire extinguishers and respirators shall be inspected at least monthly." Damaged or unserviceable equipment
must be removed from service and replaced.

## What protective equipment is required?

The protective clothing rules in 1910.156(e) apply to employees who perform interior structural fire fighting. They "do
not apply to employees who use fire extinguishers or standpipe systems to control or extinguish fires only in the
incipient stage." For those who do go inside, the employer must provide the clothing "at no cost to the employee," and it
must cover foot and leg, hand, body, and eye, face and head protection.

For breathing protection, 1910.156(f)(1)(ii) requires approved self-contained breathing apparatus (SCBA) to be "provided
to and worn by fire brigade members while working inside buildings or confined spaces where toxic products of combustion
or an oxygen deficiency may be present." Under (f)(1)(v), SCBA must have "a minimum service-life rating of 30 minutes,"
except escape units used only for emergency escape, and under (f)(1)(vi) it must have an alarm that sounds when the
remaining service life is "within a range of 20 to 25 percent of its rated service time." Under (f)(2)(i), only
pressure-demand or other positive-pressure SCBA may be worn for interior structural fire fighting.

## Is this rule being replaced?

OSHA published a proposed rule on February 5, 2024, the Emergency Response Standard, which in the Federal Register
abstract's words would "replace the existing Fire Brigades Standard." That document is a proposal, not a final rule. The
eCFR text as of November 20, 2026 still contains 1910.156 as summarized here.

## What this means for OSHA study

The points to know: 1910.156 applies when an employer establishes a fire brigade; the employer keeps a written
organizational statement; all members train at least annually and interior structural fire fighters at least quarterly;
fire fighting equipment is inspected at least annually, and portable extinguishers and respirators at least monthly; and
protective clothing and SCBA rules apply to members who fight fires beyond the incipient stage inside buildings.
