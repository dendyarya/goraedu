OSHA's construction rules give temporary heaters their own section: 29 CFR 1926.154, "Temporary heating devices," in Subpart F (Fire Protection and Prevention). This explainer
answers one practical question: what does OSHA require when a temporary heater is used on a jobsite? It reads the
section as it stands in the eCFR (text current to November 23, 2026). It is an explainer, not a rule change. It covers
construction work under Part 1926.

## Is there enough fresh air?

1926.154(a)(1) states: "Fresh air shall be supplied in sufficient quantities to maintain the health and safety of
workmen. Where natural means of fresh air supply is inadequate, mechanical ventilation shall be provided."

For confined spaces, 1926.154(a)(2) adds: "When heaters are used in confined spaces, special care shall be taken to
provide sufficient ventilation in order to ensure proper combustion, maintain the health and safety of workmen, and
limit temperature rise in the area."

The section does not set a number of air changes or a gas level; it states the purposes the ventilation must serve.

## How far from combustible material?

Under 1926.154(b)(1), "Temporary heating devices shall be installed to provide clearance to combustible material not
less than the amount shown in Table F-4." The table gives these minimum clearances in inches:

| Heating appliance               | Sides | Rear | Chimney connector |
| ------------------------------- | ----- | ---- | ----------------- |
| Room heater, circulating type   | 12    | 12   | 18                |
| Room heater, radiant type       | 36    | 36   | 18                |

A heater can be closer only if its listing allows it. 1926.154(b)(2) states: "Temporary heating devices, which are
listed for installation with lesser clearances than specified in Table F-4, may be installed in accordance with their
approval."

## Can a heater sit on a wood floor?

Only if it is suitable for that. 1926.154(b)(3) says "Heaters not suitable for use on wood floors shall not be set
directly upon them or other combustible materials. When such heaters are used, they shall rest on suitable heat
insulating material or at least 1-inch concrete, or equivalent. The insulating material shall extend beyond the heater 2
feet or more in all directions."

## What about tarps and enclosures?

For coverings such as tarps, 1926.154(b)(4) states: "Heaters used in the vicinity of
combustible tarpaulins, canvas, or similar coverings shall be located at least 10 feet from the coverings. The coverings
shall be securely fastened to prevent ignition or upsetting of the heater due to wind action on the covering or other
material."

## Does the heater have to be level?

Yes, unless the maker says otherwise. Under 1926.154(c), "Heaters, when in use, shall be set horizontally level, unless
otherwise permitted by the manufacturer's markings."

## Are salamanders allowed?

Not the solid fuel kind indoors or on scaffolds. 1926.154(d) is one sentence: "Solid fuel salamanders are prohibited in
buildings and on scaffolds."

## Rules for oil-fired heaters

1926.154(e) covers heaters that burn liquid fuel:

- "Flammable liquid-fired heaters shall be equipped with a primary safety control to stop the flow of fuel in the event
  of flame failure. Barometric or gravity oil feed shall not be considered a primary safety control." (1926.154(e)(1))
- "Heaters designed for barometric or gravity oil feed shall be used only with the integral tanks." (1926.154(e)(2))
- "Heaters specifically designed and approved for use with separate supply tanks may be directly connected for gravity
  feed, or an automatic pump, from a supply tank." (1926.154(e)(4))

Paragraph (e)(3) is reserved. Heaters that use liquefied petroleum gas also have rules in 29 CFR 1926.153,
"Liquefied petroleum gas (LP-Gas)," which this post does not cover.
