This explainer covers NABCEP's own program rules as published on the date checked above. The North American Board of
Certified Energy Practitioners (NABCEP) is a private organization, not a government agency. Its Certification Handbook
says: "NABCEP is a non-profit credentialing organization." goraedu is independent and is not affiliated with or
endorsed by NABCEP.

The practical question: what must you show before NABCEP lets you sit for its PV Design Specialist (PVDS) exam?
Nothing here describes a rule change.

NABCEP's Board Certifications page says the PVDS "recognizes the advanced experience and skill of PV system designers."
For eligibility, that page links to Section 5 of the Certification Handbook, whose cover is marked "2025 Update". The facts below come
from that section unless another one is named.

## Is the PV Design Specialist a license?

No. The handbook states: "NABCEP credentials are not professional licenses issued by a government agency" and "do not
authorize a credential holder to practice." Section 5 calls the PVDS "a voluntary Board Certification" and adds: "It
is not intended to prevent qualified individuals from designing PV systems or to replace state license requirements."
Any engineering, electrical or contractor license your state requires is a separate matter.

## What are the basic requirements?

Section 5.1 lists them. An applicant must "Be at least 18 years of age", "Meet prerequisites of related experience
and/or education", "Complete an application form documenting all requirements", "Sign and agree to uphold the NABCEP
Code of Ethics" and "Pass a written examination". It also lists paying NABCEP's application and exam fees; this post
does not restate amounts.

## What has to be documented to qualify?

Section 5.3 names three items:

- "Completion of a minimum of 10 hours of OSHA Outreach Training Program for the Construction Industry training"
  "(or government equivalent)"
- "Completion of 24 hours of advanced training as defined in Section 5.5.2 below"
- "Completion of designs for installations equaling at least 12 Project Credits"

For the OSHA course itself, see our [OSHA hub](/osha/). Section 5 does not list the PV Associate or the PV Installation Professional
as a prerequisite.

## How do Project Credits work for designs?

Credits depend on system size: "System size of 1 – 999kW = 2 Project Credits" and "System size of 1MW and up = 3
Project Credits". By our arithmetic, six designs under 1 MW would reach the 12-credit minimum.

Each design must meet Section 5.2's minimum criteria. The handbook says: "The Applicant must have designed and drawn
the plans for the installations with their name and/or initials on the drawing." The designs "must have been completed
within the two (2) calendar years prior to the application submission date." Each system must have "a minimum
rating of 1 kW DC (STC)."

## What proof goes with each design?

For each design, Section 5.4 asks for the plan set, a system summary and "documentation of approval by a governing
body." If the system was built, Section 5.4.3 asks for electrical or building permits and final approved inspection
records from the local authority having jurisdiction, and states: "Inspections may not be completed by the Applicant,
Installation Contractor or customer."

A design that was sold but never built can still count. Section 5.4.4 says the applicant then submits "a signed letter
on letterhead from the contractor, employer, or customer stating that they are satisfied with the design" "of the PV
system and the reason why the system was not completed." Section 5.4.5 adds that applicants "will be asked to
describe the scope of work they were responsible for on each design."

## What training counts toward the 24 hours?

Section 5.5.2 says: "All training must have been completed in the five (5) calendar years prior to the submission of
the application." The 24 hours break down as "18 hours of advanced PV design principles and practices" based on
NABCEP's PVDS Job Task Analysis and "6 hours of the National Electric Code (NEC)."

"Advanced" means instruction 'beyond the "basic," "fundamental," or "introductory"' level. The training must come from
one of the provider types the section lists, such as institutions accredited by an agency the U.S. Department of
Education recognizes, Department of Labor Registered Apprenticeship programs, or training institutions approved by
state contractor licensing boards. The handbook warns: "Courses offered by private training organizations or
businesses that are not accredited or otherwise recognized by a third party as described above will not be accepted."

## How long do you have once approved?

Section 14.6 says approved candidates are "eligible to take the exam for a period of One (1) year from the date of
application approval." Section 15 states that each exam other than the System Inspector exams "consists of seventy
(70) equally weighted multiple-choice questions" and that "The time limit for the examination is four (4) hours."
Once certified, Section 2.4 states: "Certification is valid for three (3) years from the date of issuance."
