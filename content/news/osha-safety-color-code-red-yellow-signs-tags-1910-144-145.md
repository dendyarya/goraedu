Red cans, yellow stripes, red-and-black danger signs: a lot of workplace safety is communicated by color. In OSHA's
general industry standards, two sections in Subpart J set those colors: 29 CFR 1910.144 for marking physical hazards
and 1910.145 for accident prevention signs and tags. This explainer reads both as they stand in the eCFR (current to
November 12, 2026). Nothing here describes a rule change.

## What does red mean under 1910.144?

1910.144(a)(1) states: "Red shall be the basic color for the identification of" three things. The first, "Fire
protection equipment and apparatus," is now marked "[Reserved]" in the eCFR, so it has no text. The other two are:

- **Danger.** "Safety cans or other portable containers of flammable liquids having a flash point at or below 80 °F,
  table containers of flammable liquids (open cup tester), excluding shipping containers, shall be painted red with
  some additional clearly visible identification either in the form of a yellow band around the can or the name of the
  contents conspicuously stenciled or painted on the can in yellow." The same paragraph adds: "Red lights shall be
  provided at barricades and at temporary obstructions. Danger signs shall be painted red."
- **Stop.** "Emergency stop bars on hazardous machines such as rubber mills, wire blocks, flat work ironers, etc.,
  shall be red." Also, "Stop buttons or electrical switches which letters or other markings appear, used for emergency
  stopping of machinery shall be red."

Note the scope of the can rule: it covers portable containers of flammable liquids with a flash point "at or below
80 °F," and it excludes shipping containers.

## What does yellow mean?

1910.144(a)(3) states: "Yellow shall be the basic color for designating caution and for marking physical hazards such
as: Striking against, stumbling, falling, tripping, and 'caught in between.'"

1910.144 itself names only red and yellow; its other paragraphs, (a)(2) and (b), are "[Reserved]."

## Which colors go on safety signs?

1910.145 sorts signs into three classes in paragraph (c) and sets their colors in (d)(2), (d)(4) and (d)(6):

| Sign                    | What it is for or means, 1910.145(c)                                                                                    | Colors, 1910.145(d)                                                                     |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Danger sign             | "danger signs indicate immediate danger and that special precautions are necessary"                            | "red, black, and white"                                                                 |
| Caution sign            | "only to warn against potential hazards or to caution against unsafe practices"                                | background "yellow; and the panel, black with yellow letters"                           |
| Safety instruction sign | "where there is a need for general instructions and suggestions relative to safety measures"                   | background "white; and the panel, green with white letters"                             |

For caution signs, "Any letters used against the yellow background shall be black." For safety instruction signs,
"Any letters used against the white background shall be black." The exact shades are those "specified in Table 1" of
ANSI Z53.1-1967 or ANSI Z535.1-2006(R2011), incorporated by reference in § 1910.6. 1910.144(a)(1)(ii) says "Danger signs
shall be painted red"; 1910.145(d)(2) gives the fuller color specification for danger signs.

1910.145(c)(1)(ii) and (c)(2)(ii) also require that employees be told what danger and caution signs mean: "All employees shall be instructed that danger
signs indicate immediate danger and that special precautions are necessary," and "All employees shall be instructed
that caution signs indicate a possible hazard against which proper precaution should be taken."

The scope note in 1910.145(a)(1) matters: the specifications "do not apply to plant bulletin boards or to safety
posters," and they are "intended to cover all safety signs except those designed for streets, highways, and railroads."
Paragraph (a)(2) adds: "All new signs and replacements of old signs shall be in accordance with these specifications."

## Do accident prevention tags have required colors?

Not under 1910.145. Under 1910.145(f)(4)(i), all required tags must contain a signal word ("Danger," "Caution," or
"Biological Hazard," "BIOHAZARD," or the biological hazard symbol) and a major message, and 1910.145(f)(7) allows
"Warning" tags for a hazard level between "Caution" and "Danger." Paragraph (f) sets no tag color. Appendix A to 1910.145(f), "Recommended Color Coding," states: "While the
standard does not specifically mandate colors to be used on accident prevention tags, the following color scheme is
recommended by OSHA":

- "DANGER": "Red, or predominantly red, with lettering or symbols in a contrasting color."
- "CAUTION": "Yellow, or predominantly yellow, with lettering or symbols in a contrasting color."
- "WARNING": "Orange, or predominantly orange, with lettering or symbols in a contrasting color."
- "BIOLOGICAL HAZARD": "Fluorescent orange or orange-red, or predominantly so, with lettering or symbols in a
  contrasting color."

1910.145(f)(1)(ii) also states that the tag paragraph "does not apply to construction or agriculture."

## What this means for OSHA study

Red stands for danger and emergency stops, yellow for caution and physical hazards, and green panels appear on safety
instruction signs. Sign colors are mandatory under 1910.145(d), while 1910.145 only recommends tag colors (Appendix A to 1910.145(f)). Our
[lockout/tagout study guide](/osha-general-industry/lockout-tagout/study-guide/) covers where tags are used in energy
control, and the [OSHA general industry hub](/osha-general-industry/) lists every topic. Our questions are our own,
