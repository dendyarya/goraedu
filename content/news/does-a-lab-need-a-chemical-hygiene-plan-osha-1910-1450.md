Many workplaces have a room where chemicals are mixed or tested. OSHA's general industry rules have a separate standard
for laboratory work: 29 CFR 1910.1450, "Occupational exposure to
hazardous chemicals in laboratories", often called the laboratory standard. This explainer answers one practical
question from the text of that section on eCFR: when does a lab need a written Chemical Hygiene Plan, and what has to
be in it? Nothing here describes a rule change.

## Which labs does the standard cover?

Paragraph (a)(1) states: "This section shall apply to all employers engaged in the laboratory use of hazardous
chemicals as defined below." The definition in paragraph (b) is narrow. "Laboratory use of hazardous chemicals" means
handling or use of such chemicals in which all four conditions are met:

- chemical manipulations are carried out on a "laboratory scale";
- "Multiple chemical procedures or chemicals are used;"
- "The procedures involved are not part of a production process, nor in any way simulate a production process; and"
- "Protective laboratory practices and equipment" are available and in common use to minimize the potential for
  employee exposure to hazardous chemicals.

"Laboratory scale" means work in which the containers "are designed to be easily and safely manipulated by one
person", and it "excludes those workplaces whose function is to produce commercial quantities of materials."

## When does it not apply?

Paragraph (a)(3) lists two exclusions. The first is chemical use that does not meet the definition above; in that case
"the employer shall comply with the relevant standard in 29 CFR part 1910, subpart Z, even if such use occurs in a
laboratory." So a room called a lab is not enough on its own. The second is "Laboratory uses of hazardous chemicals
which provide no potential for employee exposure." The rule's examples are dip-and-read test strips and commercially
prepared kits, such as those used in pregnancy tests, "in which all of the reagents needed to conduct the test are
contained in the kit."

Where the standard applies, paragraph (a)(2) states that it "shall supersede, for laboratories, the requirements of all
other OSHA health standards in 29 CFR part 1910, subpart Z", with three exceptions in (a)(2)(i) to (iii): the permissible
exposure limits still apply (unless the particular standard states otherwise); the prohibition of eye and skin contact
"shall be observed" where an OSHA health standard specifies it; and where the action level (or in its absence, the PEL)
is routinely exceeded for a substance with exposure monitoring and medical surveillance requirements, paragraphs (d) and
(g)(1)(ii) apply.

## Is the Chemical Hygiene Plan written?

Yes. Paragraph (e)(1) states that where hazardous chemicals as defined by the standard are used in the workplace, "the
employer shall develop and carry out the provisions of a written Chemical Hygiene Plan". The plan must be capable of
protecting employees from health hazards associated with hazardous chemicals in that laboratory and of keeping exposures
below the limits in paragraph (c). Under (e)(2), it "shall be readily available to employees, employee representatives
and, upon request, to the Assistant Secretary."

## What must the plan contain?

Paragraph (e)(3) lists eight elements, and the plan "shall indicate specific measures that the employer will take to
ensure laboratory employee protection":

1. Standard operating procedures relevant to safety and health for laboratory work involving hazardous chemicals.
2. Criteria for choosing control measures, including engineering controls, personal protective equipment and hygiene
   practices, with "particular attention" to chemicals known to be extremely hazardous.
3. "A requirement that fume hoods and other protective equipment are functioning properly" and specific measures to
   ensure their performance.
4. Employee information and training under paragraph (f).
5. The circumstances under which an operation, procedure or activity needs prior approval from the employer or the
   employer's designee.
6. Medical consultation and examinations under paragraph (g).
7. Designation of the people responsible for implementing the plan, "including the assignment of a Chemical Hygiene Officer and, if
   appropriate, establishment of a Chemical Hygiene Committee".
8. Additional protection for "particularly hazardous substances": "select carcinogens," reproductive toxins and
   substances with a high degree of acute toxicity. Designated areas, containment devices such as fume hoods or glove
   boxes, safe removal of contaminated waste and decontamination procedures must be considered and included "where
   appropriate".

Paragraph (e)(4) adds: "The employer shall review and evaluate the effectiveness of the Chemical Hygiene Plan at least
annually and update it as necessary." Appendix A, based on the National Research Council's recommendations, is
"non-mandatory" guidance for writing the plan.

## Who is the Chemical Hygiene Officer?

The definition in paragraph (b): "an employee who is designated by the employer, and who is qualified by training or
experience, to provide technical guidance in the development and implementation of the provisions of the Chemical
Hygiene Plan." The rule does not tie the role to a job title.

## When must employees be trained?

Under (f)(2), information and training are given "at the time of an employee's initial assignment to a work area where
hazardous chemicals are present and prior to assignments involving new exposure situations." The rule does not set a
fixed refresher interval: "The frequency of refresher information and training shall be determined by the employer."
Under (f)(4), training includes methods and observations to detect the presence or release of a hazardous chemical, the physical and health hazards of the chemicals in the work area, the
protective measures, and "the applicable details of the employer's written Chemical Hygiene Plan."

## What about exposure monitoring, medical care and records?

- **Monitoring**: under (d)(1), the employer measures exposure to a substance whose standard requires monitoring "if
  there is reason to believe that exposure levels for that substance routinely exceed the action level (or in the
  absence of an action level, the PEL)." Under (d)(4), employees are told the results in writing "within 15
  working days after the receipt of any monitoring results", either individually or by posting them in an appropriate
  location accessible to employees.
- **Medical care**: paragraph (g) gives employees an opportunity for a medical examination when they develop signs or
  symptoms of exposure in the lab, and an opportunity for a medical consultation after "a spill, leak, explosion or other
  occurrence resulting in the likelihood of a hazardous exposure". Where monitoring shows exposure routinely above the action level (or the PEL) for a substance with
  medical surveillance requirements, medical surveillance follows that substance's standard ((g)(1)(ii)). Examinations
  and consultations are performed by or under the direct supervision of a licensed physician and provided "without
  cost to the employee, without loss of pay and at a reasonable time and place."
- **Records**: under (j), the employer keeps a record of exposure measurements and of medical consultations and
  examinations for each employee, kept and made available "in accordance with 29 CFR 1910.20." Our earlier post explains
  [employee access to exposure and medical records](/news/osha-access-to-employee-exposure-and-medical-records-1910-1020/).
