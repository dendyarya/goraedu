Home inspector licensing is set by each state that regulates the work. This explainer covers **North Carolina only**.
There, G.S. 143-151.46(a) states: "The North Carolina Home Inspector Licensure Board is established in the Department of
Insurance." The Board's pages are hosted on the North Carolina Office of State Fire Marshal (OSFM) site. From the
statute (Chapter 143, Article 9F) and the Board's rules (Title 11, Chapter 8 of the North Carolina Administrative Code),
this post answers one practical question: what does a North Carolina home inspector license require? Nothing here
describes a rule change, and nothing here applies to any other state.

## Who needs the license?

G.S. 143-151.45(5) defines the term: "Home inspector. - An individual who engages in the business of performing home
inspections for compensation." A home inspection, under 143-151.45(4), is "A written evaluation, based on observation or
noninvasive testing, of two or more of the following components" of a residential building; the statute then lists the
components.

## What does the statute require?

G.S. 143-151.51(a) states: "To be eligible to be licensed as a home inspector, an applicant must do all of the
following:" The list includes "Submit a completed application to the Board upon a form provided by the Board.", "Pass a
licensing examination prescribed by the Board." and "Pay the applicable fees." It also requires meeting the insurance
rules in 143-151.51(b), covered below.

The applicant must also meet one of three qualifying routes in 143-151.51(a)(5):

- "Have a high school diploma or its equivalent and satisfactorily complete an education program approved by the
  Board." The statute adds: "The program must be completed within three years of the date the applicant submits an
  application".
- "Have education and experience the Board considers to be equivalent to that required by sub-subdivision a."
- "Be licensed for at least six months as a general contractor under Article 1 of Chapter 87 of the General Statutes";
  the same route also covers licensed architects and professional engineers.

## How long is the education program?

Under the Board's rules (11 NCAC 08 .1021), "The home inspection prelicensing education program shall consist of a
minimum total of 200 hours". It is made of "Academic education consisting of at least 120 hours classroom instruction"
and "Training consisting of at least 80 hours of field instruction with at least 10 site visits." The statute caps the
program: under G.S. 143-151.49(b), "The education program adopted by the Board may not consist of more than 200 hours of
instruction."

## What counts as equivalent experience?

Rule .1004 lists examples the Board accepts, including "A bachelor of science degree from any engineering, architecture
or building technology school and two years experience", "Six years experience as an employee who works under the
direct supervision of a licensed general contractor" and "Certification by the North Carolina Code Officials
Qualification Board as a Code Enforcement Official". Under .1004(b), "Applicants may submit other experience in the
design, installation, or inspection of buildings". For this route, .1004(c) states: "The home inspection course or
training program shall consist of at least 120 hours of instruction." Read .1004 in full for the conditions attached to
each example. The Board's application page refers applicants who use work experience to "Rule: .1004 Equivalent
Experience".

## What about the exam?

Rule .1007 states: "The examination shall be administered at least twice each year." and "The applicant must answer 70%
of the questions correctly to receive a passing grade." Under .1008, applicants return the application form and
supporting documents to the Board "at least 30 days before the next scheduled examination". The Board's application
page states: "All applicants for licensure as a home inspector must create/register an account for a user ID
assignment".

## What insurance is required?

G.S. 143-151.51(b) requires general liability insurance plus one more form of financial protection: minimum net assets,
a bond, or errors and omissions insurance, in amounts set by the statute and the Board (Rule .1006(b) sets the bond amount). Rule .1006(a) says the
liability insurance "shall cover the licensee's activities performed during the course of a home inspection on the
premises" and "shall include coverage for both property damage and bodily injury coverage". Under .1006(c), "Each
licensed home inspector shall annually submit, during the month of January, a certificate of insurance". The statute
adds in 143-151.55(d) that a license "shall lapse if the licensee fails to continuously maintain the [insurance]
requirements".

## How long does the license last?

G.S. 143-151.55(a) states: "A license expires on September 30 of each year." Renewal requires, among other things,
that the licensee "has fulfilled the continuing education requirements set by the Board". Under 143-151.64(a), annual
continuing education hours are set by the Board and "shall not be less than 12 credit hours and no more than 20 hours".

## Where to check before you act

These are the requirements in the statute and the Board's rules as published on the date above. The Board's rules
booklet says it is "an unofficial compilation" and that "the official published versions of those authorities
control." Confirm the current rules, course approvals and forms with the Board before you enroll, count experience or
apply. These rules describe North Carolina only; other states set their own. For the federal lead disclosure and radon
topics that home inspectors meet, see our [home inspector topics](/home-inspector/).
