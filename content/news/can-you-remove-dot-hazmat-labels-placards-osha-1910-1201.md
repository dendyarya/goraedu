A drum, tote or trailer that arrives with U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) hazmat labels, markings or placards
raises a practical question for the receiving workplace: may those be taken off once the shipment is on site? OSHA's
general industry rules (29 CFR Part 1910) answer it in 29 CFR 1910.1201, in Subpart Z (Toxic and Hazardous Substances).
This explainer reads every paragraph of that section from the eCFR text, which is current to November 19, 2026. It is an
explainer, not a rule change: nothing here describes a new or proposed rule.

## Who does 1910.1201 apply to?

Paragraphs (a) and (b) both turn on whether the item "is required to be" marked, labeled or placarded under the U.S. Department of
Transportation's Hazardous Materials Regulations, which paragraph (a) identifies as "49 CFR Parts 171 through 180". If
DOT's rules did not require the marking, label or placard, these two paragraphs do not reach it.

Paragraph (e) ties the vocabulary to DOT as well: "the term “hazardous material” and any other terms not defined in this
section have the same definition as in the Hazardous Materials Regulations (49 CFR Parts 171 through 180)." Words such
as "package," "non-bulk" and "transport vehicle" therefore take the meaning given in those regulations.

## Can the labels come off a drum or tote once it is received?

Not while the hazard remains. Paragraph (a) covers packages: an employer who receives a package of hazardous material
that must be marked, labeled or placarded under the DOT rules "shall retain those markings, labels and placards on the
package until the packaging is sufficiently cleaned of residue and purged of vapors to remove any potential hazards."

The end point is the condition of the packaging: an empty drum that still holds residue or vapors keeps them.

## What about a trailer, rail car or freight container?

Paragraph (b) covers the equipment a shipment arrives in. An employer who receives "a freight
container, rail freight car, motor vehicle, or transport vehicle" that must be marked or placarded under the Hazardous
Materials Regulations "shall retain those markings and placards" on it "until the hazardous materials which require the
marking or placarding are sufficiently removed to prevent any potential hazards."

Unlike paragraph (a), paragraph (b) speaks of markings and placards, not labels, and its
end point is the removal of the hazardous materials, not the cleaning and purging of the packaging.

## Do the markings have to stay visible?

Yes. Paragraph (c) is one sentence: "Markings, placards and labels shall be maintained in a manner that ensures that they
are readily visible."
It applies to markings, placards and labels alike.

## Is there an exception for small packages that stay on site?

There is one, in paragraph (d): "For non-bulk packages which will not be reshipped, the provisions of this section are met
if a label or other acceptable marking is affixed in accordance with the Hazard Communication Standard (29 CFR
1910.1200)."

Both conditions apply: the package must be non-bulk (as DOT defines it, under paragraph (e)), and it must not be
reshipped. The exception does not mention freight containers, rail cars or vehicles. It replaces the DOT markings with a Hazard Communication Standard label; it does not drop the warning.

## What does a Hazard Communication Standard label have to show?

For containers in the workplace, 1910.1200(f)(6) requires each container of hazardous chemicals to be "labeled, tagged or
marked" with either the full shipped-container label information in (f)(1)(i) through (v), or the "Product identifier
and words, pictures, symbols, or combination thereof, which provide at least general information regarding the hazards
of the chemicals," which together with the other information available under the hazard communication program gives
employees the specific hazard information. Paragraph (f)(6) applies "Except as provided in paragraphs (f)(7) and (f)(8)",
which cover stationary process containers, certain portable containers and some pharmacy-dispensed drugs.

The Hazard Communication Standard has its own rule on incoming labels as well. Under 1910.1200(f)(9), "The employer shall
not remove or deface existing labels on incoming containers of hazardous chemicals, unless the container is immediately
marked with the required information." Paragraph (f)(10) adds that workplace labels must be "legible, in English, and
prominently displayed on the container, or readily available in the work area throughout each work shift."

## What this means for OSHA study

For general industry, keep the five parts of 1910.1201 straight: (a) packages keep DOT markings, labels and placards
until cleaned of residue and purged of vapors; (b) freight containers, rail cars and vehicles keep markings and placards
until the hazardous materials are sufficiently removed; (c) they must stay readily visible; (d) non-bulk packages that
will not be reshipped may carry a Hazard Communication Standard label instead; (e) terms take their DOT meaning.

The [hazard communication study guide](/osha/hazard-communication/study-guide/) and its
[practice test](/osha/hazard-communication/practice-test/) cover 1910.1200 as part of our OSHA 10 and 30 construction
set. The general industry [hazardous materials study guide](/osha-general-industry/hazardous-materials/study-guide/)
and [practice test](/osha-general-industry/hazardous-materials/practice-test/) cover compressed gases and flammable
liquids (1910.101 and 1910.106), and the [OSHA general industry hub](/osha-general-industry/) lists the other topics. On
the DOT side, the [CDL hazmat placarding study guide](/cdl/hazmat-placarding/study-guide/) covers 49 CFR Part 172
Subpart F.
