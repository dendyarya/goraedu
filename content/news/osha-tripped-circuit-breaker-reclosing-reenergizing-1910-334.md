OSHA's general industry electrical work-practice rule 29 CFR 1910.334, "Use of equipment," in Subpart S (Electrical),
has a paragraph on what happens after a circuit breaker trips or a fuse blows. This explainer asks one question: when
may a circuit that a protective device has opened be turned back on? It describes the federal regulation as published
in the eCFR on the date checked (November 21, 2026); nothing here describes a rule change. OSHA rules are not a
license: electrician licensing is set by states, and this post does not cover any state's licensing rules or the
construction rules in Part 1926.

## Who do these work-practice rules cover?

Section 1910.331(a) states that 1910.331 through 1910.335 "cover electrical safety-related work practices for both
qualified persons (those who have training in avoiding the electrical hazards of working on or near exposed energized
parts) and unqualified persons (those with little or no such training) working on, near, or with" premises wiring, wiring
for connection to supply, other outside wiring and certain optical fiber cable. Section 1910.331(c) excludes some work
by qualified persons, such as work on generation, transmission and distribution installations.

## Can a tripped breaker be reset right away?

Not until it has been determined that it can be safely energized. Section 1910.334(b)(2), "Reclosing circuits after protective device operation,"
states: "After a circuit is deenergized by a circuit protective device, the circuit may not be manually reenergized
until it has been determined that the equipment and circuit can be safely energized."

The same paragraph continues: "The repetitive manual reclosing of circuit breakers or reenergizing circuits through
replaced fuses is prohibited."

## Is an examination always needed?

No. A note to 1910.334(b)(2) states: "When it can be determined from the design of the circuit and the overcurrent
devices involved that the automatic operation of a device was caused by an overload rather than a fault condition, no
examination of the circuit or connected equipment is needed before the circuit is reenergized."

The definitions in 1910.399 explain the difference. "Overload" is "Operation of equipment in excess of normal,
full-load rating, or of a conductor in excess of rated ampacity that, when it persists for a sufficient length of time,
would cause damage or dangerous overheating," and the definition adds: "A fault, such as a short circuit or ground
fault, is not an overload." "Overcurrent" is "Any current in excess of the rated current of equipment or the ampacity
of a conductor. It may result from overload, short circuit, or ground fault."

## What may be used to switch circuits under load?

Section 1910.334(b)(1) states: "Load rated switches, circuit breakers, or other devices specifically designed as
disconnecting means shall be used for the opening, reversing, or closing of circuits under load conditions." It adds
that "Cable connectors not of the load-break type, fuses, terminal lugs, and cable splice connections may not be used
for such purposes, except in an emergency."

Section 1910.334(b)(3) states that overcurrent protection "may not be modified, even on a temporary basis, beyond that
allowed by § 1910.304(e)," the installation requirements for overcurrent protection.

## Who may test the circuit?

Section 1910.334(c)(1) states: "Only qualified persons may perform testing work on electric circuits or equipment."
Under (c)(2), test instruments, leads, cables, power cords, probes and connectors "shall be visually inspected for
external defects and damage before the equipment is used," and if a defect or damage "might expose an employee to injury," the item "shall be removed from service" until
repaired and tested. Under
(c)(3), they "shall be rated for the circuits and equipment to which they will be connected and shall be designed for
the environment in which they will be used."

## What about a circuit that was locked out for work?

That is a different situation with its own steps. Section 1910.333(b)(2) applies "While any employee is exposed to
contact with parts of fixed electric equipment or circuits which have been deenergized." Under 1910.333(b)(2)(v),
"These requirements shall be met, in the order given, before circuits or equipment are reenergized, even
temporarily":

- "(A) A qualified person shall conduct tests and visual inspections, as necessary, to verify that all tools,
  electrical jumpers, shorts, grounds, and other such devices have been removed, so that the circuits and equipment
  can be safely energized."
- "(B) Employees exposed to the hazards associated with reenergizing the circuit or equipment shall be warned to stay
  clear of circuits and equipment."
- "(C) Each lock and tag shall be removed by the employee who applied it or under his or her direct supervision." If
  that employee is absent, a designated qualified person may remove it, provided the employer ensures the employee "is
  not available at the workplace" and "is aware that the lock or tag has been removed before he or she resumes work
  at that workplace."
- "(D) There shall be a visual determination that all employees are clear of the circuits and equipment."
