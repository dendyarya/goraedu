Most regulated drinking water contaminants have a maximum contaminant level (MCL). A few have a treatment technique
instead, and for two of them, acrylamide and epichlorohydrin, that treatment technique is a yearly written certification
under 40 CFR 141.111 rather than a sampling limit. For Drinking Water Operator Certification study, a practical question
is what exactly a public water system has to certify, and what happens if it does not.
This explainer reads that section and the rules around it as they stand in the eCFR. It does not describe a rule change,
and your State's drinking water program may add its own requirements, so check with your State as well.

## Why is there no MCL for these two?

They are covered by Subpart K of Part 141, Treatment Techniques. Section 141.110 says the Subpart K requirements
"constitute national primary drinking water regulations" and that "These regulations establish treatment techniques in
lieu of maximum contaminant levels for specified contaminants." EPA's National Primary Drinking Water Regulations table
defines a treatment technique (TT) as "A required process intended to reduce the level of a contaminant in drinking
water." In that table, both contaminants show "zero" as the MCLG and "TT" in place of an MCL.

The zero goal comes from 40 CFR 141.50(a): "MCLGs are zero for the following contaminants", a list that includes
"Acrylamide" and "Epichlorohydrin." An MCLG is a health goal; the enforceable requirement for these two is the treatment
technique.

## What exactly must the system certify?

Section 141.111 states that "Each public water system must certify annually in writing to the State (using third party
or manufacturer's certification) that when acrylamide and epichlorohydrin are used in drinking water systems, the
combination (or product) of dose and monomer level does not exceed the levels specified as follows":

- "Acrylamide = 0.05% dosed at 1 ppm (or equivalent)"
- "Epichlorohydrin = 0.01% dosed at 20 ppm (or equivalent)"

The section ends: "Certifications can rely on manufacturers or third parties, as approved by the State."

Four points come straight from that text:

- **Who:** "Each public water system". The section names no system type or size exception.
- **When it applies:** "when acrylamide and epichlorohydrin are used in drinking water systems".
- **How often and to whom:** "annually in writing to the State".
- **What it is based on:** the "combination (or product) of dose and monomer level", supported by a manufacturer's or
  third party's certification, as approved by the State.

EPA's table words the same requirement with the dose in mg/L ("0.05% dosed at 1 mg/L (or equivalent)" and "0.01%
dosed at 20 mg/L (or equivalent)") and notes that milligrams per liter "are equivalent to parts per million (PPM)."

## Where do these chemicals come from?

EPA's table lists the source of acrylamide in drinking water as "Added to water during sewage/wastewater treatment" and
the sources of epichlorohydrin as "Discharge from industrial chemical factories; an impurity of some water treatment
chemicals". Section 141.111 itself does not name products or say which treatment chemicals contain them; it sets the
dose and monomer limits and the certification.

## Is there a sampling requirement?

Section 141.111 sets no sampling schedule. Appendix A to Subpart Q, which assigns public notice tiers, lists "Acrylamide
(TT)" and "Epichlorohydrin (TT)" under "Other Treatment Techniques" with a citation of 141.111 for a treatment technique
violation, and "N/A" in the column for monitoring and testing procedure violations.

## What happens if the requirement is violated?

Appendix A assigns Tier 2 public notice to a treatment technique violation for each of the two contaminants. Section
141.203(b)(1) states that public water systems "must provide the public notice as soon as practical, but no later than
30 days after the system learns of the violation." Subpart Q sets further conditions on Tier 2 notices, such as a possible primacy agency extension, repeat
notice and how the notice is delivered, which this post does not cover.

Section 141.205(d)(1) adds that systems "must include in each public notice the health effects language specified in
appendix B to this subpart corresponding to each MCL, MRDL, and treatment technique violation listed in appendix A to
this subpart". For acrylamide, Appendix B reads:
"Some people who drink water containing high levels of acrylamide over a long period of time could have problems with
their nervous system or blood, and may have an increased risk of getting cancer." For epichlorohydrin: "Some people who
drink water containing high levels of epichlorohydrin over a long period of time could experience stomach problems, and
may have an increased risk of getting cancer."
